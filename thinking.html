Thinking POV

Build First, Apologize Later

Nobody asked me to build it.

There was a problem — every time a professional at my firm got featured in a news article, someone had to manually read the piece, fill out a standardized format, and make sure we weren't copy-pasting content that could create legal issues. It was repetitive, it was slow, and it was the kind of task that falls to whoever has a free hour.

I had a free hour. I also had Claude.

So I built a skill — a reusable AI workflow that reads the article, categorizes it accurately, extracts the right information into the standardized format, and hard-checks for fabricated quotes. I used it for weeks before I told anyone. The output was clean. The categories were right. No hallucinated quotes, no invented attribution.

Then I showed the team on a call.

The first reaction was skepticism — fair. AI making up quotes in a professional services context is a real risk, not a hypothetical one. But the output spoke for itself. The team saw it work in real time, understood how the guardrails functioned, and within weeks it was running in production.

That's the thing nobody tells you about being entry-level: you're not too junior to solve problems. You're actually positioned perfectly to solve them. You see the inefficiencies with fresh eyes. You haven't been doing it the old way long enough to think the old way is inevitable. And you have access to tools that can close the gap between "this is annoying" and "this is automated" faster than any generation before you.

Most professionals have settled into knowing what they do and how they do it. You know what they do — and you can do it better and faster.

So find the problem. Sign up to be the solution. Build the thing. Use it until the results are undeniable. Then show the team.

Ask permission to scale. Not to build.

Thinking POV

Search is Dead. Is Your Content?

Nobody is Googling your blog post.

They're asking ChatGPT. They're asking Perplexity. They're asking Claude. And if your brand isn't showing up in those answers — you don't exist. Not to that person. Not in that moment. Not for that decision.

This isn't a prediction anymore. In May 2026, Google's VP of Search stood on stage at I/O and said it plainly: "Google Search is AI search." TechCrunch called it the same week: "The era of the ten blue links is officially over." The game changed. Most marketing teams are still playing the old one.

We spent a decade optimizing for a blue link. Keywords, backlinks, meta descriptions, H1 tags — all of it built for a search engine that shows ten results and hopes you pick the right one. That game isn't over, but it's not the only game anymore.

Here's what actually changed: search engines rank pages. AI systems cite sources. The difference is enormous.

To rank, you need authority and relevance. To get cited, you need to be the clearest, most direct answer to the question someone is actually asking. AI doesn't scroll. It doesn't compare. It synthesizes — and it reaches for the source that made the answer obvious.

That means your 2,000-word SEO blog written for a crawl bot? Probably not getting cited. Your clearly structured, direct, expertise-first content that actually answers the question? That's the new real estate.

And while we're here — nobody has a full attention span anymore unless you hand them a reason to. Have a report? Break it into micro content for social. Have a blog? It exists for one reason now: to build AI authority. To signal to the systems that synthesize answers that you are a credible source worth citing.

If you don't show up in AI responses, who are you?

The teams winning right now aren't the ones with the biggest content budgets. They're the ones who figured out that the algorithm changed — and changed with it.
